\subsection{Market Research Report}

\subsubsection{Research Scope and Approach}
This section asks one question: is there a real, unmet need that a UV-sensitive
patch paired with a phone app can address? To answer it, we drew on health
authorities (WHO and the American Academy of Dermatology), Vietnamese weather and
news agencies, consumer surveys, and market data providers. Our target customers
are young urban adults aged 18 to 35 who already use sunscreen and carry a
smartphone, the same group used to size the market in
Section~\ref{sec:market-evaluation}.

One limitation should be stated up front. We found good data on how often people
reapply sunscreen in the United States and New Zealand, but no published survey of
reapplication habits in Vietnam. We therefore treat those studies as indicative
and combine them with Vietnamese data on UV levels, sunscreen use, and smartphone
ownership. Confirming the behavior with Vietnamese users is a next step for the
team.

\subsubsection{Strong but Invisible UV Exposure}
Ultraviolet radiation can neither be seen nor felt, yet it is the main cause of
skin cancer and also leads to sunburn, DNA damage, and premature skin aging.
According to the WHO, more than 1.5 million skin cancer cases were diagnosed and
over 120,000 skin cancer deaths were reported worldwide in 2020 \cite{whofact}.

Vietnam sits in the tropics, so the exposure is high for most of the year. On
24 May 2023, the national forecasting center reported midday UV index values of
9.1 in Ho Chi Minh City and between 9.1 and 10 in Ha Long, Hai Phong, Hue, Hoi An,
and Nha Trang, while Hanoi stayed at the ``high'' level of 5.5 to 7.4
\cite{vna2023} (Figure~\ref{fig:uv-vietnam}). In Vietnamese reporting, a ``very
high'' reading of 7.5 to 10.4 can cause sunburn after about 25 minutes of
unprotected exposure, and readings of 10.5 or more after about 15 minutes
\cite{vietnamnet}.

An honest reading of the health data also needs one caveat. Skin cancer is
relatively uncommon in Vietnam: the IARC estimated only 157 new melanoma cases in
2018 \cite{nguyen2020}. For most young Vietnamese users, the everyday motivation
is therefore not fear of cancer alone, but protecting the look of their skin.
Surveys of Vietnamese consumers show that the main worry about sun exposure is
skin damage, and that people aged 25 and over also worry about wrinkles and aged
skin \cite{qandmetan}.

\subsubsection{Customer Needs and Behavior}
Sun protection is already part of daily life for many Vietnamese consumers,
especially women. In a 2026 online survey of 200 residents of Hanoi and Ho Chi
Minh City aged 20 to 49, 76\% of women said they apply sunscreen, compared with
45\% of men, and use was higher among high-income respondents (70\%) than
low-income ones (52\%) \cite{qandme2026}. A study of 590 patients at the National
Hospital of Dermatology in Hanoi found a similar split, with 75.7\% of women and
13.0\% of men using sunscreen (42.6\% overall) \cite{nguyen2020}. That sample
consists of hospital patients, so it should not be read as the general population.
Awareness is not the problem either: in an earlier Q\&Me survey of 500 Vietnamese
adults, 88\% knew about UV and 84\% said they cared about it \cite{qandme2014}.

Putting this together, our target customers do not need another sunscreen. They
need to know when the sunscreen on their skin has stopped doing its job. In
practice this means three needs:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0pt]
  \item \textbf{Feedback:} a clear signal for whether protection is still working.
  \item \textbf{Convenience:} a solution that adds almost no effort to a daily
    routine of studying, commuting, or exercising outdoors.
  \item \textbf{Affordability:} a price that students can pay for regular use.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Pain Points}
\textbf{UV gives no warning.} Because UV is invisible, people often use the wrong
signals. In a US survey of regular sunscreen users, 45\% of parents said they use
``redness'' as the cue to reapply on their children \cite{fct2023}, which means
the skin is already damaged by the time they act.

\textbf{People know the rule but do not follow it.} The AAD recommends reapplying
sunscreen every two hours and after swimming or sweating \cite{aad2023}. In an AAD
survey of 1,000 US adults, 80\% knew this, yet only 33\% typically reapplied
every two hours, 42\% either did not reapply at all or only when they got wet, and
30\% applied sunscreen only to the face \cite{aad2021}. A nationally
representative survey in New Zealand found that just 13\% reapply every two hours
and 9\% never do; among the reasons for not always reapplying, nearly half (45\%)
said they simply forget \cite{castles2023} (Figure~\ref{fig:forget}). A
peer-reviewed study of 2,283 regular sunscreen users in the United States
likewise found that between 20\% and 60\% do not reapply, depending on the product
type \cite{fct2023}.

\textbf{Everyday life makes a two-hour rule hard to follow.} Students in class,
commuters on motorbikes, and runners on the road are unlikely to track the time
since their last application, so a fixed schedule is easy to forget.

\textbf{Existing tools do not close the gap.} As Section~\ref{sec:competitors}
shows, the closest sticker product costs around VND 415,000 per 16-pack and has no
phone app, a hardware UV clip-on was discontinued because of its price, and free
UV apps only show forecasts or timers without checking the sunscreen on the skin.

\subsubsection{Potential Demand}
Demand for sun care in Vietnam is growing. Section~\ref{sec:market-size} shows that
the sun care market is forecast to grow from USD 124.12 million in 2022 to
USD 244.79 million by 2030, a CAGR of 8.86\% \cite{marketsdata2023}. Uvie targets
the part of that market that already buys sunscreen and is open to a tool that
makes it work better, rather than people who do not use sun protection at all.

The digital side of the opportunity is also in place. Vietnam had 85.6 million
internet users in October 2025 \cite{datareportal2026}, and 91.3\% of mobile
internet subscriptions were on smartphones \cite{vnnews2026}. A camera-based web
app can therefore reach the target group without any extra hardware.

These numbers show that the need and the channel exist. They do not yet show how
many people would pay for the patch; that estimate is made in
Section~\ref{sec:market-size} and tested financially in Section~\ref{sec:financial}.

\subsection{Justification for the Selected Market Opportunity}
We chose this opportunity for four reasons.

\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=0pt]
  \item \textbf{The need is constant and local.} Vietnamese cities regularly reach
    ``very high'' UV levels (Figure~\ref{fig:uv-vietnam}), so the need for reliable
    protection is not seasonal or occasional.
  \item \textbf{The gap is in behavior, not in products.} People already buy
    sunscreen and know the rules, but they forget or misjudge when to reapply
    (Figure~\ref{fig:forget}). Uvie adds the missing feedback and works alongside
    sunscreen brands instead of competing with them.
  \item \textbf{Existing alternatives leave room.} Current options are expensive,
    lack a phone app, require extra devices, or do not check the sunscreen on the
    skin (Section~\ref{sec:competitors}).
  \item \textbf{We can build it with accessible technology.} The solution combines
    a UV-sensitive photochromic patch with a free camera-based web app, so users
    need no new device. At a direct cost of VND 34,000 against a VND 89,000 retail
    price, the unit economics in Section~\ref{sec:financial} leave a gross margin of
    about 62\%.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Assumptions still to be validated.} The opportunity rests on several
assumptions that this desk research cannot settle on its own:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0pt]
  \item Vietnamese users reapply sunscreen at least as rarely as the US and New
    Zealand samples suggest.
  \item The patch color change tracks real sunscreen wear-off (see the testing in
    Section~\ref{sec:prototype}).
  \item Target customers are willing to pay VND 89,000 per package and repurchase
    about three times a year.
  \item The product meets the regulatory requirements for skin-contact materials
    (see Section~\ref{sec:financial}).
\end{itemize}
