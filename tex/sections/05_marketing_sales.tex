\section{Marketing and Sales Plan}\label{sec:marketing}

This section explains how Uvie will enter the market, win its first customers, and
grow. The plan has three parts: a marketing strategy that decides whom to target and
what to say, a sales process that turns attention into purchases and repeat purchases,
and a set of measures to check whether it is working. All volumes and budgets match
the financial projection in Section~\ref{sec:financial}.

%====================================================================
\subsection{Marketing Strategy}

\subsubsection{Goals}
The plan has one main goal for each year, taken from the financial forecast:
Year~1 reaches 12,000 new customers and sells 18,000 packages in Ho Chi Minh City;
Year~2 reaches 30,000 new customers and 50,000 packages by adding Hanoi, Da Nang,
and pharmacies; Year~3 reaches 60,000 new customers and 100,000 packages through
nationwide beauty chains and repeat buyers. These targets equal 0.13\%, 0.37\%, and
0.74\% of the serviceable market (Table~\ref{tab:market-size}), which is small enough
to be realistic for a new brand.

\subsubsection{Segmentation, Targeting, and Positioning}
\textbf{Segmentation.} We divide sunscreen users by what they do outdoors and how they
shop: students and young workers who commute and study in the sun, outdoor sports
users who sweat and stay out for hours, and parents who buy for children.

\textbf{Targeting.} We start with two groups. Students and young workers aged 18 to
35 in Ho Chi Minh City are the core, because they are online, price-sensitive, and
easy to reach through campuses and short videos. Runners and cyclists are the early
adopters whose reviews will be the most credible to everyone else.

\textbf{Positioning.} For young urban adults who already wear sunscreen, Uvie is the
affordable patch-and-app that shows when it is time to reapply. Unlike expensive
imported sticker packs, hardware UV gadgets, or forecast apps that never look at the
skin, Uvie checks the sunscreen you are actually wearing.

\subsubsection{Promotion and Content}
Our target customers spend their time on short-video and social platforms, so our
promotion is built there. In a 2026 survey of Vietnamese app users, 82\% had used
TikTok in the previous seven days \cite{qandmeapps2026}, and Vietnam had 85.6 million
internet users in late 2025 \cite{datareportal2026}. TikTok Shop already held 41.31\%
of online platform sales in 2025, second only to Shopee at 56.04\% \cite{krasia2026}.
A video can therefore show the product, and the viewer can buy it in the same app. Our own
survey of 40 people points the same way: 72.5\% said they would prefer to buy on Shopee or TikTok
Shop (Appendix~\ref{app:survey}).

The content plan has four simple formats:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0pt]
  \item \textbf{The wipe demo.} A 15-second video: apply sunscreen over the patch,
    stay in the sun, wipe the sunscreen off, and watch the patch turn dark. It shows
    the whole idea without a word of explanation.
  \item \textbf{Today's UV.} A daily post with the local UV level and a reminder to
    reapply, linking to the web app. UV levels in Vietnamese cities are often
    ``very high'' (Figure~\ref{fig:uv-vietnam}), which gives the content a constant
    reason to exist.
  \item \textbf{Creator and student reviews.} Runners, cyclists, and student
    ambassadors try the patch for a week and report honestly.
  \item \textbf{Sports and campus events.} Free trial patches at running events and on
    campuses. Field studies in Australia handed out UV stickers at sports events and
    festivals for the same reason \cite{horsham2020}.
\end{itemize}

Marketing claims must stay within what the product can show. Uvie reminds people to
reapply; it does not promise protection from skin cancer or sunburn. This also keeps
us clear of rules on health advertising (see Section~\ref{sec:financial}).

\subsection{Sales Process}

\subsubsection{Three Stages of Market Entry}
Following the course approach \cite{aulet2017}, we divide the sales process into
three stages: in the short term we show what the product is and create demand, in
the medium term we refine the product and build production and sales capacity, and
in the long term we scale. This also follows the logic of moving from early
adopters to the mainstream market \cite{moore2014}. Table~\ref{tab:phases} sets out
the plan.

highest at the start because the brand is unknown. Later, repeat buyers and

We will connect the web app and each storefront to web analytics so that every step
of the funnel can be counted and the weakest step can be fixed first. The app's free
demo mode is the key sales tool at the \emph{Ask} step because it lets a doubtful
customer test the idea at no cost.

\subsubsection{Customer Acquisition Cost}
The cost of acquiring a customer is the marketing and sales budget divided by the
number of new customers. In Year~1, VND 360 million for 12,000 customers gives
VND 30,000 per customer. Figure~\ref{fig:cac} shows the plan: the number of new
customers grows fivefold over three years while the cost of reaching each one falls
by almost half, to VND 16,667, as referrals, brand awareness, and retail partners
take over part of the work. This is an assumption and not a result. If the real cost
stays at the Year~1 level, the LTV-to-CAC ratio in Table~\ref{tab:ltvcac} stays at
11.0 instead of rising to 19.8. Repeat purchase matters more: if customers bought only
once, lifetime gross profit would be VND 55,000 and the Year~1 ratio would drop to
about 1.8.

\subsection{Pricing, Promotions, and Customer Service}
\textbf{Pricing.} Uvie uses a low-price entry strategy: VND 89,000 per package, with
the same list price in every channel. The price covers the VND 34,000 direct cost
and leaves a gross profit of VND 55,000 (Table~\ref{tab:unit}). The number of patches
in each package is still to be fixed after supplier tests, and the price will be
reviewed once it is.

\textbf{Promotions.} We plan a few simple offers, not constant discounting, since
heavy discounts lower the price customers expect and eat into a margin we need for
marketing: a starter offer for first-time buyers, a multi-package bundle for regular
users, and a referral reward for both the existing customer and the friend. The
exact size of each offer will be set by testing in Year~1.

\textbf{Customer service.} Because the product is a reminder, trust is everything.
We answer questions about how to use the patch quickly through chat, collect
photographs of scans that gave a confusing result, and use them to improve the app.

\textbf{Repeat purchase.} The business depends on customers buying again. We will
ask buyers who use the app regularly if they want a reminder when they are likely to
run out, and we will offer refill bundles. This feature does not exist in the MVP
and is part of the roadmap (Section~\ref{sec:prototype}).

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=0pt]
  \item \textbf{Weak repeat purchase.} If customers use the patch once and stop, the
    plan fails. We would respond with refill bundles, reminders, and more
    outdoor-sports partners.
  \item \textbf{Rising platform costs.} Online sales are concentrated in two
    platforms \cite{krasia2026}, so advertising prices or fees can change quickly.
    We reduce the risk by building our own audience through the app and social pages.
  \item \textbf{Trust in the product.} A patch that gives wrong signals would damage
    the brand. Testing before launch (Section~\ref{sec:prototype}) and careful claims
    are our main protection.
\end{itemize}
